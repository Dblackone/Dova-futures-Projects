# The tender evaluation report

A document for a decision-maker who will not open the workbook. It must carry
its own reasoning, and it must be honest about what it does not know.

Write in continuous prose with tables where tables help. Avoid bullet-point
soup — a procurement decision worth millions deserves argument, not fragments.

## Structure

```
Title block            Subject, existing installation, scope, submissions
                       received, bid range, currency, status line
Executive summary
1. Basis and scope     1.1 Stated basis of evaluation
                       1.2 Work performed / not performed
2. The tender pack
3. Commercial comparison   3.1 As quoted
                           3.2 Normalisation to like-for-like
                           3.3 Preliminaries, overhead and profit
4. Independent price verification
5. Specification defects
6. Condition of the existing installation   (where relevant)
7. Assessment of the equipment platform
8. Observations by submission
9. Preliminary ranking     9.1 Nature of this ranking
10. Recommendations        10.1, 10.2 as needed
11. Limitations
Appendix A — technical schedule for bidders to return
```

## Title block

A two-column table of facts, ending with a status line that sets expectations:
*"For Client decision — not a technical or safety certification."*

That line does real work. It stops the report being cited later as something it
never claimed to be.

## Executive summary

The most important page. Lead with the finding, not the process.

State the price spread and then explain what actually causes it. On our tender:
*"That spread is not explained by commercial competitiveness. It is explained by
the bidders having priced materially different things."*

Give the two or three findings that must be resolved before award, each in a
paragraph. Then the ranking table. Then the recommendation, stated as a cost
comparison the reader can weigh: *"The cost of doing so is a short delay. The
cost of not doing so is a contract in which neither party knows what is being
bought."*

## 1.1 Stated basis of evaluation

Where the evaluation rests on a premise you have not verified, record it here
rather than burying it.

Distinguish sharply between accepting a premise and finding it true:

> For the purpose of this evaluation it has been assumed that the existing main
> control panel is no longer serviceable. This is a stated assumption, not a
> finding of this review. No functional testing was carried out. The assumption
> is recorded here so that it is visible to any subsequent reader, and so that
> the conclusions which depend on it can be revisited if the premise changes.

Then say what accepting the premise does *not* carry with it. Accepting that a
panel needs replacing does not settle how many expansion panels, loop cards or
network cards go with it — those turn on architecture, not condition.

## 1.2 Work performed

Two lists: what you did, and what you did not do. The second list matters more.
It is the boundary of the report, and writing it early stops the document being
read as something broader than it is.

## 3.3 Preliminaries, overhead and profit

Two tables, and the second one is the point.

First, the professional services line alone as a percentage of equipment. Then
immediately warn that it is misleading on its own, and give the second table
adding containment and builders' work. Show how the ranking changes.

Close with the observation that makes it land: the physical work is identical
across every submission — the same devices, the same cable, the same
commissioning — so a threefold spread on identical labour is a more revealing
question to put to bidders than any equipment price.

## 5. Specification defects

A table: item number, the defect, and the consequence with the recommended
correction. Quantify each — units at stake, money at stake.

Where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, say so and decline to conclude:
*"This review has insufficient information to conclude either way, and does not
do so. The requirement should be justified by the system designer."* A reviewer
who declines to guess is more useful than one who guesses confidently.

## 8. Observations by submission

One paragraph per bidder, each balanced. Lead with what is good about the
submission, then what is questionable. A bidder who stated real part numbers
when nobody else did deserves that said, even if their pricing raises questions.

Where findings are serious but not disqualifying, say which: *"These are matters
for clarification rather than automatic grounds for exclusion, and the
submission should remain under consideration pending the responses."*

## 9.1 Nature of this ranking

Never let a preliminary ranking be mistaken for a recommendation to award. State
that it is preliminary, list what must happen before it can be final, and say
plainly that the responses may change it.

## 10. Recommendations

A numbered table of actions with a rationale column, ordered so the reader can
execute top to bottom. Put the pass-or-fail conditions before the price
questions — certification before cost, always.

Where a recommendation asks for something the client's own document already
required, say so. Asking a bidder to comply with an existing obligation is a
stronger position than asking for new work, and the client should know they are
standing on the stronger ground.

## 11. Limitations

Market prices are indicative, not offers. Exchange rates move. Identifications
made from photographs are provisional. Quantities are the client's and
unverified. No assessment was made of any bidder's competence or standing. This
is a commercial and specification review, not an engineering certification.

Close by saying that recommendations requiring specialist judgment should be
validated by a qualified professional. That is not a hedge — it is the accurate
boundary of a desk review, and stating it protects the client as much as you.

## Appendix A — technical schedule

The highest-value single item in the whole report: a form every bidder must
return, one row per field, with a column explaining *why* each field is
required. Explaining the why raises the quality of what comes back.

Fields worth requiring: manufacturer; part number or order code; variant and
options; configuration details for assembled items; declaration of performance
or equivalent certification reference; country of manufacture; written
confirmation the goods are new and from an authorised channel; warranty per line
item; delivery lead time per line item.

Do not ask for serial numbers at tender stage — they exist only once goods are
manufactured and allocated. Make a serial number schedule a condition of
handover instead, recorded in the O&M manual where it supports warranty claims.
Asking for the impossible costs you credibility on everything else in the form.
